\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:background-and-related-work}

This chapter places the thesis within the existing research by reviewing four separate streams that are all relevant to this work. First, classical approaches to \gls{acr:ic} are described as they provide the theoretical foundation and are directly relevant to the baselines used in this work. Subsequently, the single- and multi-agent \gls{acr:rl} literature is reviewed to demonstrate the value of directly learning replenishment policies and to explore the agent decomposition that the thesis uses. Finally, literature on\gls{acr:ps} and structural adaptations to heterogeneity are explored as they provide the methodological basis for this work. Finally, the gaps that the thesis addresses are derived.\\

\section{Classical Approches to Inventory Control}
\label{sec:rw-classical}

Stochastic demand models form the analytical core of modern inventory control theory. Its formal study was initiated by the seminal work of \citet{arrow1951newsvendor}, who formalized the widely-known newsvendor problem in an inventory context. In the basic single-period model, a decision maker must choose an order quantity for a perishable product before observing realized demand and \citet{arrow1951newsvendor} show that the optimal order quantity is given by a closed-form critical-fractile solution that balances overage and underage costs. To extend the problem to a dynamic, multi-period setting with fixed ordering costs, \citet{arrow1951newsvendor} assumed a simple $(s, S)$ rule that triggers a replenishment up to $S$ once inventory drops to $s$ and minimized the resulting long-run expected loss numerically, without establishing optimality of $(s, S)$ rules in general. Later, \citet{scarf1960sSoptimality} proved that the $(s, S)$ approach is optimal for finite horizons through the introduction of $K$-convexity. Additionally, \citet{karlin1960sSoptimality} studied the special case of no fixed ordering cost and established that under non-stationary demand distributions the $(s, S)$ rule collapses into a so-called base-stock, or $(S-1, S)$, policy, on which much of the later literature builds. Following these foundational structural proofs, subsequent research relaxed the remaining assumptions of the standard model by considering lost-sales \citep{karlin1958lostsales}, stochastic-lead-times \citep{kaplan1970stochasticleadtimes}, and data-driven approaches \citep{burnetas2000datadriven,levi2007datadriven, huh2009datadriven}. More recently, data-driven approaches have been extended through feature-based and prescriptive formulations \citep{ban2019bigdata, bertsimas2020prescriptive} as well as through base-stock heuristics and learning algorithms with provable performance guarantees \citep{xin2021cappedbasestock, zhang2020learning}. 

While the previous advances considerably extend the classical model, they all remain single-location. To address this, \citet{clark1960multiechelon} laid the theoretical groundwork for the use of base-stock policies in multi-location settings by showing that echelon base-stock policies are optimal for serial and finite systems without fixed costs. This result was later extended to the infinite horizon case \citep{federgruen1984multiechelon} and to
convergent assembly systems \citep{rosling1989multiechelon}. For divergent distribution
structures, which are closest to the single-echelon distribution setting studied in this
thesis, \citet{eppen1981multiechelon} achieves analytical tractability through an allocation
assumption. Moreover, \citet{sherbrooke1968multiechelon} introduced the METRIC model for divergent networks of recoverable items, which was later complemented by the simple recursive cost-evaluation procedures of \citet{axsater1990multiechelon}.

Despite their elegance, these analytical results remain applicable only under restrictive assumptions \citep{dekok2018multiechelon}. To address this, simulation optimization has emerged as the standard alternative that
fixes a parametric policy class in advance and tunes it by repeated evaluation on a simulation model. Most such work relies on metaheuristics. Particularly, while genetic algorithms have been used most widely to determine base-stock and related order-policy parameters in multi-echelon supply chains \citep[e.g.,][]{kimbrough2002multiechelon, daniel2005multiechelon, daniel2006multiechelon}, other less-frequent approaches include fuzzy set theory \citep{petrovic1999multiechelon, giannoccaro2003multiechelon} and ranking and selection with sample average approximation \citep{tsai2013multiechelon}. A more recent line replaces the many simulation runs with fitting a statistical surrogate of the cost surface \citep[e.g.,][]{jalali2017kriging, liu2024surrogate}. Similarly, \citet{kiuchi2020bayesian} tune multi-echelon base-stock policies through \gls{acr:bo} of a supply chain simulator, which is the approach that the central baseline of this thesis is based on.\\


\section{Single-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Inventory Control}
\label{sec:rw-rl}
\gls{acr:rl} has become an attractive data-driven alternative to model-based heuristics since it can learn replenishment policies for complex stochastic systems without exact knowledge of the underlying model. \citet{giannoccaro2002inventory} were one of the first to use \gls{acr:rl} for inventory management by using a tabular approach to learn a near-optimal reorder policy for a single-item three-stage supply chain.
Later studies extended the idea to a range of network structures such as two-echelon supply chains \citep{kim2005adaptive, kim2008asynchronous}, multi-echelon settings \citep{chaharsooghi2008reinforcement, kwak2009situation, sun2012analyses}, and the multiple-retailer case \citep{dogan2015reinforcement}. 

A limitation shared by these early works is their reliance on tabular value representations such that scalability is limited \citep{boute2022roadmap}. More recently, deep \gls{acr:rl} methods addressed this issue by approximating the value function through a \gls{acr:nn} to enable the agent to generalize
across unseen states and scale to high-dimensional state and action spaces. \citet{oroojlooyjadid2022dqn} applied the popular deep Q-network algorithm of \citet{Mnih2015} on the beer game and used transfer learning to retrain it efficiently across cost configurations. Moreover, \citet{gijsbrechts2022candrl} provided a rigorous analysis of deep \gls{acr:rl} by showing that a tuned deep asynchronous advantage actor-critic agent can match the performance of well-established heuristics for the three inventory settings of lost sales, dual-sourcing, and multi-echelon. Further benchmarks on non-stationary demand \citep{dehaybe2024nonstationary} and one-warehouse multi-retailer distribution
networks \citep{kaynov2024owmr} followed. Other work focused on policy-gradient methods that parameterize the policy directly. \citet{vanvuchelen2020ppo} applied the popular \gls{acr:ppo} algorithm \citep{schulman2017} to the joint
replenishment problem in which several items share a common ordering setup. \citet{geevers2024ppo} extend the use of \gls{acr:ppo} to multi-echelon networks of different topologies with a continuous action space and report improvements over benchmark policies. Finally, \citet{vanvuchelen2024continuous} scaled \gls{acr:ppo} to large multi-dimensional action spaces by rescaling a continuous policy output to feasible integer orders.\\ 

\section{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Inventory Management}
\label{sec:rw-marl}
The single-agent methods reviewed so far all treat the entire inventory system as one central controller whose joint action space grows exponentially with the size of the system. This not only scales poorly but is often not directly applicable in practice where supply chains are typically run by many actors who decide independently from local information. \gls{acr:marl} offers a natural remedy to these concerns by distributing the decision making across several agents that learn concurrently in a shared environment \citep{Stefanomarlbook}. However, this introduces the key challenges of environment non-stationarity and learning stability such that multiple \gls{acr:marl} algorithms that address these issues were developed. As a naive foundation, \citet{dewitt2020independent} showed that \gls{acr:ippo} can match or exceed more elaborate methods on cooperative benchmarks even though every agent optimizes only its own clipped objective from local observations. \citet{yu2022surprising} introduced \gls{acr:mappo} by applying a centralized value function that is used only during training to reduce non-stationarity and reported that this approach competes with established off-policy algorithms across several cooperative settings. \citet{fu2021asyncmappo} later rebuilt \gls{acr:mappo} around an asynchronous distributed architecture so that it trains with large batches and reaches state-of-the-art results on hard cooperative tasks.

Due to its success, \gls{acr:marl} has become an increasingly popular paradigm for inventory management and it directly motivates the agent decomposition that this thesis studies. \citet{fuji2018dwe} assigned an actor-critic agent to each stage of the beer distribution game and used an evolutionary weight update to show that this decomposition can beat expert human play. Moreover, \citet{sultana2020reinforcement} scaled the idea to a two-echelon supply chain with hundreds of products by sharing network weights across products within each store and \citet{mousa2024analysis} used \gls{acr:mappo} to show that a centralized critic recovers most of the performance of a fully centralized solution. Finally, \citet{khirwar2023cooperative} considered a one-warehouse, multiple-stores setting where the warehouse has to ration scarce stock among the stores and demonstrated that the warehouse learns an effective sub-policy in this setting.  

A different set of approaches considers not the warehouses but each product as an individual agent. \citet{ding2022sharedresources} formalized the replenishment of a large product portfolio as a shared-resource stochastic game and proposed a decentralized \gls{acr:ppo} agent per product that remains sample efficient through a shared context. Additionally, \citet{leluc2023marlim} trained an \gls{acr:ippo} agent per item on a single-echelon system with stochastic demand and lead times where items compete for storage and \citet{yang2023mabim} released a multi-echelon simulator in which every product at every warehouse is an agent for benchmarking \gls{acr:marl} against classical policies.

More recently, \citet{liu2024multiechelonmarl} investigated the case where agents are not interchangeable by applying heterogeneous-agent \gls{acr:ppo} and reported that sharing information only during training weakens the bullwhip effect. This result was reinforced by \citet{wang2025symbiotic} who compared homogeneous agents that share one policy against heterogeneous agents that each keep their own and found that only the heterogeneous structure reduces the bullwhip effect. Further works combined graph neural networks with \gls{acr:mappo} to capture spatial dependencies \citep{kotecha2025gnn} or used value decomposition methods to decompose the joint value function into agent wise components for large scale multi-echelon systems \citep{zhou2024spareparts}.\\



\section{Parameter Sharing and Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:ps-heterogeneity}

Several of the \gls{acr:marl} methods reviewed above already touch on whether the agents should share a single policy network or keep separate ones. This design choice is known as \gls{acr:ps} and was introduced to cooperative deep \gls{acr:marl} by \citet{gupta2017cooperative}, who represented every agent with one network and showed that it scales learning to dozens of cooperating agents. Because a single shared network must nonetheless serve agents that may need to act differently, \citet{terry2020revisiting} formalized the common remedy of agent indication in which a fixed identifier is appended to each observation and proved that a shared policy augmented in this way can still converge to an optimal joint policy. Thus, \gls{acr:ps} ensures both efficiency of having a single network and the ability to specialize, which is why it underlies the shared learners that this thesis studies.

This efficiency of \gls{acr:ps} rests on the assumption that the agents are homogeneous enough for one network to fit them all. \citet{christianos2021scaling} showed that applying \gls{acr:ps} indiscriminately can instead degrade performance and proposed to partition the agents into groups by their abilities and goals so that only sufficiently similar agents share a network. \citet{tessera2025hypermarl} likewise adopted a shared network but generated agent-specific parameters from an agent-conditioned hypernetwork so that the policy can express distinct behavior while avoiding the gradient interference that arises when the agent identifier is coupled to the observation. Other work removes the shared network entirely. For instance, \citet{kuba2022trust} equipped each agent with its own policy and derived a trust-region update that retains a monotonic improvement guarantee without requiring the agents to share parameters.

The sharing scheme is not the only part of a shared learner that the literature adapts to heterogeneous or structured problems. A first point of adaptation is the observation. Since the natural scale of an input can vary widely, \citet{vanhasselt2016learning} rescaled the learning targets adaptively so that a single network can cope with returns that span several orders of magnitude and the large study of \citet{andrychowicz2020matters} found that normalizing the observations is among the design choices that most reliably improve on-policy learning. 

The learning signal is another area that is adapted to heterogeneous settings. Since a single reward mixes several contributions, \citet{foerster2018counterfactual} estimated a separate counterfactual advantage for each agent to alleviate credit assignment. Similarly, value decomposition methods factor a joint value into per-agent terms \citep[e.g.,][]{sunehag2018value, rashid2018qmix} and \citet{van2017hybrid} decomposed the reward into components and learned a separate value function for each so that every term becomes easier to approximate.

Finally, the network architecture is likewise adapted to heterogeneous settings, which becomes relevant whenever the observation itself has entity structure. Instead of reading one flat vector, \citet{zaheer2017deep} characterized networks whose output does not depend on the ordering of a set of inputs and \citet{zambaldi2019deep} used self-attention to reason over the entities of a scene inside a model-free policy. Similarly, \citet{iqbal2019actor} considered a multi-agent setting and placed a shared attention mechanism in the critic so that each agent attends only to the other agents that are relevant to it and \citet{hu2021updet} replaced the fixed input layout with a transformer over entity tokens so that one architecture serves a varying number of entities.\\

\section{Research Gaps}
\label{sec:rw-gap}

On balance, the reviewed literature have moved inventory control from classical single-location models toward learned policies for networks of interacting agents.
This thesis adopts the multi-agent formulation with parameter-sharing but differs from prior work in three distinct properties of the setting. First, the network structures reviewed so far are almost always generic benchmarks such as the beer game or a symmetric one-warehouse multiple-retailer system, whereas the distribution network considered in this work is based on the operations of a real firm and therefore exhibits a structure that these standard topologies do not capture. Second, prior work typically represents demand as an aggregate quantity per period. Real distribution networks rarely meet this assumption, which is why we adopt a more realistic order-level demand process. Finally, earlier multi-agent inventory studies are conducted almost exclusively under symmetric settings in which the agents face statistically identical operating conditions. We depart from this view and focus on heterogeneity that exists in the environment that the agents act in and ask whether a shared policy breaks once heterogeneity enters through the problem setting and which targeted changes recover the lost performance. The remainder of this thesis takes up this question.\\
