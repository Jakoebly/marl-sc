\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background-and-related-work}

Since its formal inception in the early twentieth century, the inventory control problem has undergone considerable methodological development from closed-form deterministic solutions to modern data-driven and learning-based approaches. This section provides the methodological background for the remainder of this thesis and reviews the relevant body of literature. While \autoref{sec:classical-inventory-control} reviews classical approaches to inventory control, \autoref{sec:rl-inventory-control} turns to reinforcement learning and discusses how data-driven methods address settings in which the assumptions of classical models no longer apply.\\

\section{Classical Approaches to Inventory Control}
\label{sec:classical-inventory-control}

While early research in inventory management focused on deterministic single-location formulations, models advanced toward stochastic multi-echelon networks that are increasingly capable of capturing the complexity of real supply chains. The following section outlines this development and highlights the foundational contributions along the two complementary axes of demand assumptions and system structure.

\paragraph{\textbf{Deterministic Foundations}} Deterministic models form the historical foundation of inventory control theory. One of the earliest formal treatments of the problem is generally attributed to \citet{harris1913eoq}, who introduced what later became known as the \gls{acr:eoq} model. The model determines the optimal order quantity by balancing ordering costs against linear holding costs for a single product under the assumptions of constant and known demand, instantaneous single-batch replenishment, fixed price and cost parameters, and no shortages. The same formula was subsequently refined and applied to a production management context by \citet{camp1922eoq} and \citet{andler1929eoq} and gained broader practical visibility through the work of \citet{wilson1934eoq}, who systematized and popularized it in operations management practice. 

Despite its simplicity, the model has proven remarkably influential and laid the groundwork for subsequent work. A first natural extension was introduced by \citet{taft1918eoqextension}, who developed the \gls{acr:epq} model by relaxing the assumption of instantaneous replenishment and considering a finite production rate. In addition, \citet{wagner1958eoqextension} addressed time-varying demand and proposed an exact dynamic programming solution. Further contributions extended the basic model by considering planned backorders, quantity discounts, deterioration, and multi-product settings with shared ordering costs \citep[e.g.,][]{hadley1963eoqextension, ghare1963eoqextension, crowther1964eoqextension, goyal1977eoqextension}. 

While these extensions remain widely used in both theory and practice \citep{shekarian2017eoquse, alnahhal2024eoquse}, they all rest on the assumption that demand is known in advance. However, it is clear that real-world inventory systems are rarely deterministic, which directly motivates the necessity for models that incorporate environment uncertainty.\\

\paragraph{\textbf{Stochastic Demand Models}}
Stochastic demand models form the analytical core of modern inventory control theory. While the first formal treatment of an inventory problem under uncertainty is commonly traced back to \citet{edgeworth1888newsvendor}, who used the central limit theorem to determine optimal cash reserves against random withdrawals, the modern formulation of the newsvendor problem in the context of inventory management relates to \citet{arrow1951newsvendor}. In its basic single-period form, a decision maker must choose an order quantity for a perishable product before observing realized demand. In turn, he either incurs an underage cost for each unit of unmet demand or an overage cost for each unit of leftover stock. \citet{arrow1951newsvendor} showed that the optimal order quantity for this single-period model follows a closed-form critical-fractile solution that balances both costs against each other and depends only on their ratio and on the demand distribution. The authors further embed this static problem into a dynamic context, in which replenishment decisions are made repeatedly over a planning horizon. In fact, when inventory carries over from one period to the next and a fixed ordering cost is present, the cost function loses both convexity and separability, which breaks the marginal-balance argument behind the critical-fractile formula and rules out any closed-form solution. To make the problem tractable, \citet{arrow1951newsvendor} assumed a simple $(s, S)$ rule that triggers a replenishment up to $S$ once inventory drops to $s$ and minimized the resulting long-run expected loss numerically, without establishing optimality of $(s, S)$ rules in general.

The conjecture that an $(s, S)$ rule is in fact optimal in this dynamic inventory problem problem motivated a substantial stream of subsequent research. \citet{scarf1960sSoptimality} provided the first rigorous proof of optimality for the finite-horizon case with linear holding and shortage costs and a fixed ordering cost. Specifically, he introduced $K$-convexity that bounds the deviation from the convexity inequality by $K$ and proved that because this property is preserved under the dynamic-programming recursion, the optimal ordering decision takes the $(s, S)$ form in every period. \citet{iglehart1963sSoptimality} extended this result to the infinite-horizon case by showing that the finite-horizon optimal policies converge to a stationary $(s, S)$ policy as the horizon grows. Similarly, \citet{karlin1960sSoptimality} studied the special case of no fixed ordering cost and established that under non-stationary demand distributions the $(s, S)$ rule collapses into a base-stock policy, which is also known as a $(S-1, S)$ policy that orders up to a single critical level $S$ whenever inventory falls below it. \citet{veinott1965sSoptimality} generalized this finding to a multi-product, infinite-horizon setting with nonstationary costs and demand, delivery lags, and discounting. Building on these structural results, further studies focused on the efficient computation of the optimal $(s, S)$ levels \citep[e.g.,][]{veinott1965computingsS, federgruen1984computingsS, zheng1991computingsS}.

Subsequent research has relaxed many of the central assumptions of the standard newsvendor problem. Distribution-free formulations \citep[e.g.,][]{scarf1958distributionfree, gallego1993distributionfree, gallego2001distributionfree} drop the requirement of a fully known demand distribution and instead optimize against the worst case over a moment-based ambiguity set. Further extensions cover lost sales \citep{karlin1958lostsales},
perishable goods \citep{fries1975perishable, nahmias1975perishable}, stochastic lead times \citep{kaplan1970stochasticleadtimes}, and capacitated replenishment \citep{federgruen1986capacitatedreplenishment}. More recently, data-driven and censored-demand variants such as those of \citet{burnetas2000datadriven, levi2007datadriven}, and \citet{huh2009datadriven} replace distributional knowledge with sample information.

Although the contributions discussed so far provide a solid theoretical framework for inventory control under uncertainty, the underlying models are limited to a single stocking location. To accurately capture real distribution systems, the inclusion of network considerations into these frameworks is thus necessary.

\paragraph{\textbf{Multi-Location and Networked Systems}}
In multi-echelon settings, replenishment decisions at different stocking locations interact through delivery lead times, capacity constraints, and shared sources of supply, which makes the joint determination of order policies considerably more challenging than in the single-stage case. The first rigorous treatment of such a setting is generally attributed to \citet{clark1960multiechelon}, who studied finite-horizon multi-echelon systems with stochastic demand, linear holding and backorder costs and showed that for serial systems and in the absence of fixed costs echelon base-stock policies are optimal. \citet{federgruen1984multiechelon} extended this result to the infinite-horizon case under both discounted and average-cost criteria. 

Since then, networked inventory models have been extended to richer network structures and to different sets of assumptions. \citet{rosling1989multiechelon} showed that any convergent assembly system under proportional costs and a long-run balance condition on initial inventories reduces to an equivalent serial system so that the echelon base-stock structure remains optimal. For divergent distribution systems \citet{eppen1981multiechelon} introduced their allocation assumption that permits negative shipments and thereby restores analytical tractability for multi-warehouse systems under normal demand. Moreover, \citet{sherbrooke1968multiechelon} introduced the METRIC model for continuous-review divergent networks of expensive low-demand recoverable items under one-for-one replenishment, which was later complemented by the simple recursive cost-evaluation procedures of \citet{axsater1990multiechelon} that underpin much of the subsequent heuristic literature.
A related line of work enriches these networked models with operational flexibility measures such as lateral transshipments, which redistribute stock proactively or reactively between locations of the same echelon to buffer local demand shocks \citep[see][]{paterson2011multiechelon}.

Despite the elegance of these analytical results, they rest on restrictive assumptions and remain provably optimal only for systems with infinite capacity, no fixed lot sizes, constant or exponential lead times, and stationary demand \citep{dekok2018multiechelon}. Once such assumptions are relaxed the curse of dimensionality of the underlying dynamic program quickly renders exact methods intractable. To address this gap, a substantial line of literature has developed around simulation-based optimization, in which a parametric policy class such as echelon base-stock levels is fixed in advance and its parameters are tuned through repeated simulation. While gradient-based methods estimate cost-function derivatives directly from sample paths \citep[e.g.,][]{glasserman1995multiechelon, woerner2018multiechelon}, derivative-free approaches instead search the parameter space directly without gradient information. Examples include metaheuristics such as genetic algorithms \citep{daniel2005multiechelon, daniel2006multiechelon, kimbrough2002multiechelon}, fuzzy-set theory \citep{petrovic1999multiechelon, giannoccaro2003multiechelon}, or ranking-and-selection procedures \citep{tsai2013multiechelon}. These methods relax the structural assumptions of the analytical literature but typically rely on extensive parameter tuning and provide no guarantee of optimality. 

Taken together, the classical literature on multi-echelon inventory control offers a rich but fragmented toolbox, in which exact analytical results are restricted to stylized structures and computational methods scale only with substantial tuning effort. This motivates the use of learning-based approaches as a natural alternative, which are the subject of the next section.\\
