\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background-and-related-work}

Since its formal inception in the early twentieth century, the inventory control problem has undergone considerable methodological development from closed-form deterministic solutions to modern data-driven and learning-based approaches. This section provides the methodological background for the remainder of this thesis and reviews the relevant body of literature. While \autoref{sec:classical-inventory-control} reviews classical approaches to inventory control, \autoref{sec:rl-inventory-control} turns to reinforcement learning and discusses how data-driven methods address settings in which the assumptions of classical models no longer apply.\\

\section{Classical Approaches to Inventory Control}
\label{sec:classical-inventory-control}

While early research in inventory management focused on deterministic single-location formulations, models advanced toward stochastic multi-echelon networks that are increasingly capable of capturing the complexity of real supply chains. The following section outlines this development and highlights the foundational contributions along the two complementary axes of demand assumptions and system structure.

\paragraph{\textbf{Deterministic Foundations}} Deterministic models form the historical foundation of inventory control theory. One of the earliest formal treatments of the problem is generally attributed to \citet{harris1913eoq}, who introduced what later became known as the \gls{acr:eoq} model. The model determines the optimal order quantity by balancing ordering costs against linear holding costs for a single product under the assumptions of constant and known demand, instantaneous single-batch replenishment, fixed price and cost parameters, and no shortages. The same formula was subsequently refined and applied to a production management context by \citet{camp1922eoq} and \citet{andler1929eoq} and gained broader practical visibility through the work of \citet{wilson1934eoq}, who systematized and popularized it in operations management practice. 

Despite its simplicity, the model has proven remarkably influential and laid the groundwork for subsequent work. A first natural extension was introduced by \citet{taft1918eoqextension}, who developed the \gls{acr:epq} model by relaxing the assumption of instantaneous replenishment and considering a finite production rate. In addition, \citet{wagner1958eoqextension} addressed time-varying demand and proposed an exact dynamic programming solution. Further contributions extended the basic model by considering planned backorders, quantity discounts, deterioration, and multi-product settings with shared ordering costs \citep[e.g.,][]{hadley1963eoqextension, ghare1963eoqextension, crowther1964eoqextension, goyal1977eoqextension}. 

While these extensions remain widely used in both theory and practice \citep{shekarian2017eoquse, alnahhal2024eoquse}, they all rest on the assumption that demand is known in advance. However, it is clear that real-world inventory systems are rarely deterministic, which directly motivates the necessity for models that incorporate environment uncertainty.\\

\paragraph{\textbf{Stochastic Demand Models}}
Stochastic demand models form the analytical core of modern inventory control theory. While the first formal treatment of an inventory problem under uncertainty is commonly traced back to \citet{edgeworth1888newsvendor}, who used the central limit theorem to determine optimal cash reserves against random withdrawals, the modern formulation of the newsvendor problem in the context of inventory management relates to \citet{arrow1951newsvendor}. In its basic single-period form, a decision maker must choose an order quantity for a perishable product before observing realized demand. In turn, he either incurs an underage cost for each unit of unmet demand or an overage cost for each unit of leftover stock. \citet{arrow1951newsvendor} showed that the optimal order quantity for this single-period model follows a closed-form critical-fractile solution that balances both costs against each other and depends only on their ratio and on the demand distribution. The authors further embed this static problem into a dynamic context, in which replenishment decisions are made repeatedly over a planning horizon. In fact, when inventory carries over from one period to the next and a fixed ordering cost is present, the cost function loses both convexity and separability, which breaks the marginal-balance argument behind the critical-fractile formula and rules out any closed-form solution. To make the problem tractable, \citet{arrow1951newsvendor} assumed a simple $(s, S)$ rule that triggers a replenishment up to $S$ once inventory drops to $s$ and minimized the resulting long-run expected loss numerically, without establishing optimality of $(s, S)$ rules in general.

The conjecture that an $(s, S)$ rule is in fact optimal in this dynamic inventory problem problem motivated a substantial stream of subsequent research. \citet{scarf1960sSoptimality} provided the first rigorous proof of optimality for the finite-horizon case with linear holding and shortage costs and a fixed ordering cost. Specifically, he introduced $K$-convexity that bounds the deviation from the convexity inequality by $K$ and proved that because this property is preserved under the dynamic-programming recursion, the optimal ordering decision takes the $(s, S)$ form in every period. \citet{iglehart1963sSoptimality} extended this result to the infinite-horizon case by showing that the finite-horizon optimal policies converge to a stationary $(s, S)$ policy as the horizon grows. Similarly, \citet{karlin1960sSoptimality} studied the special case of no fixed ordering cost and established that under non-stationary demand distributions the $(s, S)$ rule collapses into a base-stock policy, which is also known as a $(S-1, S)$ policy that orders up to a single critical level $S$ whenever inventory falls below it. \citet{veinott1965sSoptimality} generalized this finding to a multi-product, infinite-horizon setting with nonstationary costs and demand, delivery lags, and discounting. Building on these structural results, further studies focused on the efficient computation of the optimal $(s, S)$ levels \citep[e.g.,][]{veinott1965computingsS, federgruen1984computingsS, zheng1991computingsS}.

Subsequent research has relaxed many of the central assumptions of the standard newsvendor problem. Distribution-free formulations \citep[e.g.,][]{scarf1958distributionfree, gallego1993distributionfree, gallego2001distributionfree} drop the requirement of a fully known demand distribution and instead optimize against the worst case over a moment-based ambiguity set. Further extensions cover lost sales \citep{karlin1958lostsales},
perishable goods \citep{fries1975perishable, nahmias1975perishable}, stochastic lead times \citep{kaplan1970stochasticleadtimes}, and capacitated replenishment \citep{federgruen1986capacitatedreplenishment}. More recently, data-driven and censored-demand variants such as those of \citet{burnetas2000datadriven, levi2007datadriven}, and \citet{huh2009datadriven} replace distributional knowledge with sample information.

Although the contributions discussed so far provide a solid theoretical framework for inventory control under uncertainty, the underlying models are limited to a single stocking location. To accurately capture real distribution systems, the inclusion of network considerations into these frameworks is thus necessary.

\paragraph{\textbf{Multi-Location and Networked Systems}}
In multi-echelon settings, replenishment decisions at different stocking locations interact through delivery lead times, capacity constraints, and shared sources of supply, which makes the joint determination of order policies considerably more challenging than in the single-stage case. The first rigorous treatment of such a setting is generally attributed to \citet{clark1960multiechelon}, who studied finite-horizon multi-echelon systems with stochastic demand, linear holding and backorder costs and showed that for serial systems and in the absence of fixed costs echelon base-stock policies are optimal. \citet{federgruen1984multiechelon} extended this result to the infinite-horizon case under both discounted and average-cost criteria. 

Since then, networked inventory models have been extended to richer network structures and to different sets of assumptions. \citet{rosling1989multiechelon} showed that any convergent assembly system under proportional costs and a long-run balance condition on initial inventories reduces to an equivalent serial system so that the echelon base-stock structure remains optimal. For divergent distribution systems \citet{eppen1981multiechelon} introduced their allocation assumption that permits negative shipments and thereby restores analytical tractability for multi-warehouse systems under normal demand. Moreover, \citet{sherbrooke1968multiechelon} introduced the METRIC model for continuous-review divergent networks of expensive low-demand recoverable items under one-for-one replenishment, which was later complemented by the simple recursive cost-evaluation procedures of \citet{axsater1990multiechelon} that underpin much of the subsequent heuristic literature.
A related line of work enriches these networked models with operational flexibility measures such as lateral transshipments, which redistribute stock proactively or reactively between locations of the same echelon to buffer local demand shocks \citep[see][]{paterson2011multiechelon}.

Despite the elegance of these analytical results, they rest on restrictive assumptions and remain provably optimal only for systems with infinite capacity, no fixed lot sizes, constant or exponential lead times, and stationary demand \citep{dekok2018multiechelon}. Once such assumptions are relaxed the curse of dimensionality of the underlying dynamic program quickly renders exact methods intractable. To address this gap, a substantial line of literature has developed around simulation-based optimization, in which a parametric policy class such as echelon base-stock levels is fixed in advance and its parameters are tuned through repeated simulation. While gradient-based methods estimate cost-function derivatives directly from sample paths \citep[e.g.,][]{glasserman1995multiechelon, woerner2018multiechelon}, derivative-free approaches instead search the parameter space directly without gradient information. Examples include metaheuristics such as genetic algorithms \citep{daniel2005multiechelon, daniel2006multiechelon, kimbrough2002multiechelon}, fuzzy-set theory \citep{petrovic1999multiechelon, giannoccaro2003multiechelon}, or ranking-and-selection procedures \citep{tsai2013multiechelon}. These methods relax the structural assumptions of the analytical literature but typically rely on extensive parameter tuning and provide no guarantee of optimality. 

Taken together, the classical literature on multi-echelon inventory control offers a rich but fragmented toolbox, in which exact analytical results are restricted to stylized structures and computational methods scale only with substantial tuning effort. This motivates the use of learning-based approaches as a natural alternative, which are the subject of the next section.\\

\section{Reinforcement Learning for Inventory Control}
\label{sec:rl-inventory-control}

Reinforcement learning offers a fundamentally different route to the inventory control problem than the classical approaches reviewed above. The analytical and simulation-based methods of \autoref{sec:classical-inventory-control} share two structural commitments. First, they restrict attention to a parametric policy class such as base-stock or $(s, S)$, which is provably optimal only under narrow conditions and degrades to a heuristic once those conditions are relaxed. Second, they compute the parameters of that class offline from an assumed demand distribution and then hold them fixed, so the resulting policy cannot adapt to demand that is non-stationary, censored, or unknown. Beyond these two commitments lies the curse of dimensionality that renders the exact dynamic program intractable. Reinforcement learning addresses all three issues at once. It learns a control policy directly from interaction, it optimizes the full sequential cost without committing to a predetermined structural form, and it replaces the exact value recursion by a learned approximation.

\paragraph{\textbf{From Dynamic Programming to Learned Policies}}
The conceptual link between the dynamic programming of \autoref{sec:classical-inventory-control} and modern reinforcement learning runs through approximate dynamic programming, also termed neuro-dynamic programming by \citet{bertsekas1996ndp}. The central idea is to approximate the value function of an intractable dynamic program by a parametric function estimated from simulated trajectories rather than to solve the Bellman recursion exactly. \citet{vanroy1997ndp} provided an early demonstration of this idea in an inventory context. They applied neuro-dynamic programming to a retailer inventory problem whose thirty-three state variables place it well beyond the reach of exact dynamic programming and reported inventory cost reductions of roughly ten percent over an optimised order-up-to policy. This study established the template that much of the subsequent literature would follow. A high-dimensional inventory problem is cast as a Markov decision process and its policy is learned through simulation.

\paragraph{\textbf{Early Reinforcement Learning for Supply Chain Ordering}}
A first wave of studies applied tabular reinforcement learning to supply chain ordering and established its feasibility on small problems. \citet{giannoccaro2002rl} modelled a three-stage chain as a semi-Markov decision process and learned a near-optimal reorder policy under an average-reward criterion using the SMART algorithm. Their learned policy generalised the $(s, S)$ form by letting both control levels vary with the global inventory position and it outperformed a static periodic-order benchmark. \citet{chaharsooghi2008beergame} addressed the four-echelon beer game through Q-learning over a shared coded state and reported that the learned ordering mechanism beats both a one-for-one policy and a genetic-algorithm baseline under stochastic demand and lead times. Related contributions carried these ideas to non-stationary demand and to richer behavioural settings \citep[e.g.,][]{mortazavi2015selfadaptive, valluri2009rl}.

These early results came at a cost that limited their reach. Tabular methods require the state and action spaces to be discretized, so their memory and sample requirements grow rapidly as the problem expands. \citet{valluri2009rl} reported that independent learners in a multi-stage chain need thousands of periods to stabilize, and \citet{vantongeren2007qlearning} found that a single Q-learning agent in a competitive beer game reduces the bullwhip effect yet neither attains the optimal policy nor converges reliably once several agents learn simultaneously. This last observation is an early signal that placing independent learners in the same network is not a straightforward extension of single-agent learning, a point that \autoref{sec:marl} takes up in full.

\paragraph{\textbf{Deep Reinforcement Learning}}
The adoption of neural function approximation removed the discretisation ceiling and shifted the literature toward problems for which no good heuristic is known. \citet{oroojlooyjadid2022beergame} trained a deep Q-network on the beer game and used transfer learning to retrain it efficiently across cost configurations. \citet{gijsbrechts2022drl} provided the first rigorous benchmark of deep reinforcement learning on three classical and intractable problems, namely lost sales, dual sourcing, and multi-echelon inventory management, and showed that a tuned asynchronous advantage actor-critic agent matches the performance of problem-specific state-of-the-art heuristics. \citet{vanvuchelen2020ppo} applied proximal policy optimisation to the joint replenishment problem and reported that the learned policy approaches the optimal structure and surpasses established heuristics. Subsequent work extended deep policies to multi-echelon ordering \citep{kurian2022drl}. Across this literature proximal policy optimisation has emerged as the default backbone for inventory control because it remains stable under the large action spaces and high stochasticity that these problems exhibit, which is also the reason the present thesis builds on the PPO family.

This progress is not unqualified. \citet{boute2022roadmap} surveyed the design choices that govern a deep reinforcement learning implementation for inventory control and cautioned that the approach is computationally demanding, sensitive to its hyperparameters, and at times still outperformed by well-tuned heuristics. The sensitivity to hyperparameters in particular recurs throughout the empirical literature and motivates explicit tuning controls in any comparative study that attributes a performance gap to anything other than the search over hyperparameters.

\paragraph{\textbf{Toward Multiple Learning Agents}}
A feature common to the studies reviewed in this section is that they control the network through a single decision process. Either one policy selects the joint order vector for the entire chain, or one node learns while the remaining nodes follow fixed rules. Both arrangements become problematic as the network grows. A single joint controller faces an action space that expands combinatorially in the number of locations, and it presupposes centralised access to the full network state that real distribution systems rarely provide. Decentralised operation, in which each location acts on its own local information, is the realistic default rather than the exception. These two pressures are precisely what multi-agent reinforcement learning is designed to address, namely the combinatorial growth of the joint action space and the decentralised nature of operational information. \autoref{sec:marl} turns to this setting.

\section{Reinforcement Learning for Inventory Control}
\label{sec:rl-inventory-sdcontrol}

The limitations of the classical toolbox outlined in \autoref{sec:classical-inventory-control} have, since the late 1990s, motivated a complementary stream of literature that recasts inventory control as a sequential decision problem under uncertainty and approaches it through }. Rather than seeking closed-form policies under restrictive structural assumptions, this body of work learns replenishment rules directly from interaction with a simulated or real environment. The following section outlines the development of this literature, from early tabular formulations to modern \textbf{{acr:drl}} methods, and identifies the structural limitations that motivate the multi-agent perspective adopted in \autoref{sec:marl-inventory-control}.

\paragraph{\textbf{From Dynamic Programming to Reinforcement Learning}}
Almost every inventory control problem of practical interest can be cast as a \textbf{{acr:mdp}} in which a decision maker observes a state describing on-hand inventory, pipeline orders, and possibly demand features, selects a replenishment action, and incurs a stage cost composed of holding, shortage, ordering, and transportation terms. In principle, the optimal policy for any such \textbf{{acr:mdp}} can be computed by backward induction on the Bellman equation \citep{bellman1957dp}. As already noted at the end of the previous section, however, exact dynamic programming is rendered intractable by the joint curses of dimensionality of the state, action, and outcome spaces as soon as one departs from stylized structures \citep{powell2011adp}. \textbf{{acr:rl}} \citep{sutton2018rl} addresses these curses by replacing exact value iteration with sample-based updates and by approximating the value function or policy with parametric function approximators, which allows the dimensionality of the representation to be decoupled from the size of the underlying state space. The combination of \textbf{{acr:rl}} with deep neural networks \citep{mnih2015dqn} extended this idea to high-dimensional, continuous, and only partially observed state representations and ultimately gave rise to the \textbf{{acr:drl}} methods that dominate the contemporary inventory literature.

\paragraph{\textbf{Tabular and Early Approximate Approaches}}
Early applications of \textbf{{acr:rl}} to inventory control predate the deep learning era and rely on tabular value-function representations or low-dimensional linear approximators. \citet{vanroy1997neurodp} provided one of the first demonstrations that a neuro-dynamic programming approach can yield near-optimal replenishment policies for a multi-echelon retailer-warehouse system in which exact dynamic programming is intractable. Building on this idea, a stream of work explored Q-learning and SARSA in stylized supply-chain settings, most prominently the beer distribution game and small serial systems \citep[e.g.,][]{giannoccaro2002rl, kim2008rl, chaharsooghi2008rl, jiang2009rl, mortazavi2015rl}. These studies established the feasibility of learning replenishment rules directly from simulated trajectories and showed that learned policies can rival or outperform $(s,S)$ and base-stock heuristics in settings with non-stationary demand or behavioral counterparties. Nevertheless, the reliance on discretized state and action spaces and the limited expressive power of tabular and linear approximators confined them to small problems and motivated the move to deep function approximation.

\paragraph{\textbf{Deep Reinforcement Learning for Inventory Control}}
The introduction of the Deep Q-Network of \citet{mnih2015dqn} catalyzed a rapid transfer of \textbf{{acr:drl}} techniques to inventory management. \citet{oroojlooyjadid2022dqn} were among the first to bring this paradigm into the inventory literature with a Deep Q-Network agent for the beer game and showed that, even when the remaining stages follow irrational policies, the learned policy attains near-optimal cost without access to demand distributions or system parameters. Shortly thereafter, \citet{gijsbrechts2022a3c} provided the first systematic benchmark of \textbf{{acr:drl}} against state-of-the-art heuristics across three classical and intractable inventory problems\textemdash lost sales, dual sourcing, and serial multi-echelon\textemdash using the Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic algorithm \citep{mnih2016a3c}. Their study established two findings that have since shaped the field: \textbf{{acr:drl}} can match or exceed the performance of carefully tuned heuristics across heterogeneous problem classes, but only at the cost of considerable hyperparameter tuning and computational effort. These results triggered a broad wave of subsequent work that applies value-based and actor-critic methods to richer settings, including perishable inventory \citep[e.g.,][]{selukar2022perishable}, joint pricing and replenishment \citep[e.g.,][]{liu2023jointpricing}, and \textbf{{acr:iot}}-driven replenishment under seasonality.

\paragraph{\textbf{Policy-Gradient Methods and Continuous Replenishment}}
A recurring observation across these studies is that the discrete action spaces inherited from value-based \textbf{{acr:drl}} scale poorly with the range of admissible order quantities and become prohibitive in multi-product or multi-location systems. Policy-gradient methods, which directly parameterize the replenishment policy and admit continuous action spaces, have therefore emerged as the dominant algorithmic family in the recent inventory literature. In particular, Proximal Policy Optimization \citep{schulman2017ppo} has become the de facto standard owing to its sample efficiency, training stability, and limited sensitivity to hyperparameters. \citet{vanvuchelen2020ppo} applied \textbf{{acr:ppo}} to the joint replenishment problem and demonstrated that a single learned policy can adapt to a wide range of demand and cost configurations. \citet{geevers2024ppo} subsequently showed that \textbf{{acr:ppo}} consistently outperforms benchmark heuristics across linear, divergent, and general multi-echelon networks, including a real-world case study, with only minor problem-specific modifications. \citet{vanvuchelen2024continuous} pushed this line of work further by introducing continuous action representations that scale \textbf{{acr:drl}} to inventory systems with several thousand stocking locations, addressing one of the central scalability bottlenecks of the discrete-action paradigm.

\paragraph{\textbf{Hybrid and Structure-Exploiting Approaches}}
Despite these advances, generic \textbf{{acr:drl}} algorithms ignore the substantial structural knowledge accumulated by decades of classical inventory theory and tend to be sample-inefficient when training from scratch. A growing body of work therefore embeds problem structure into the learning pipeline. One line of research warm-starts \textbf{{acr:drl}} agents from base-stock or $(s,S)$ policies, or shapes rewards using analytical lower bounds, in order to accelerate convergence and stabilize training \citep[e.g.,][]{kaynov2024structured}. A second line couples mathematical programming with \textbf{{acr:rl}}, using optimization subroutines to constrain the action space or to provide imitation targets \citep{harsha2022mathprogrl}. A third, end-to-end direction differentiates through the inventory dynamics themselves, allowing demand forecasts and replenishment policies to be learned jointly under a single loss \citep{madeka2022endtoend}. These hybrid approaches narrow the gap between data-driven flexibility and the structural guarantees of classical theory and have, in several cases, produced the strongest published results on standard benchmarks.

\paragraph{\textbf{Limitations of the Single-Agent Paradigm}}
Taken together, the literature reviewed above demonstrates that \textbf{{acr:drl}} can deliver high-quality replenishment policies in settings well beyond the reach of exact dynamic programming. At the same time, virtually all of this work models the entire supply chain as a single \textbf{{acr:mdp}} controlled by a centralized agent that observes the global state and dictates the orders of every stocking location. This monolithic view is increasingly difficult to sustain. First, the joint state and action spaces grow combinatorially in the number of locations, products, and lead-time slots, so that even modern policy-gradient methods face a scalability wall once realistic networks are considered. Second, real distribution systems are inherently distributed: stocking locations are operated by different organizational units, observe only local inventory and demand signals, and may be unwilling or unable to share information centrally. Third, the centralized formulation cannot represent strategic interactions between self-interested actors, which are central to many contemporary supply-chain settings. These limitations motivate a shift from single- to multi-agent formulations, in which each stocking location is modeled as an autonomous learner that interacts with the rest of the network through its actions and observations.
