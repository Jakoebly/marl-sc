\section{Environment Design}
\label{sec:environment-design}
To establish a foundation for the experiments in this chapter, we instantiate the distribution network of \autoref{chap:problem-formulation} as a concrete family of five environments. Specifically, we first introduce a baseline environment in \autoref{subsec:symmetric-reference} and then define four heterogeneous variants in \autoref{subsec:heterogeneous-variants} that each modify the baseline along a single controlled dimension. All five environments share the demand model of \autoref{sec:demand-model}, the fulfillment and cost structure of \autoref{sec:fulfillment} and \autoref{sec:costs}, and the action, reward, and initialization rules of \autoref{chap: methodology} such that all variants differ only in the parameter values reported below.\\

\subsection{Symmetric Reference}
\label{subsec:symmetric-reference}
We define a symmetric baseline environment that serves as the control against which all later experiments are measured. It removes all heterogeneity such that warehouses, regions, and \glspl{acr:sku} are perfectly interchangeable and permuting any of the warehouse, region, or \gls{acr:sku} indices leaves the joint distribution of demand, costs, and dynamics unchanged. Because it is fully symmetrical, this environment thus acts as the natural control for isolating the effect of each heterogeneity type introduced in \autoref{subsec:heterogeneous-variants}. We summarize the specific parameters that define our symmetric environment in \autoref{tab:env-symmetric} and motivate the choice of each parameter in the remainder of this section.



\paragraph{\textbf{Constants.}} Several parameters are held fixed across all five environments. Throughout our experiments, we consider a network that consists of three warehouses, three regions, and five \glspl{acr:sku}. Three warehouses is the smallest count at which the greedy allocator of \autoref{sec:fulfillment} produces non-trivial transshipments since it must rank and split orders across competing warehouses rather then relying on a single forced fallback. Five \glspl{acr:sku} on the other hand gives a portfolio that is wide enough to span a realistic light-to-heavy product range when we vary weights later. Additionally, we standardize two temporal constants across all environments. First, we fix the lead time at $L_{ik}=3$ for every warehouse and \gls{acr:sku} so that lead-time stochasticity does not confound any heterogeneity dimension. Second, we set each episode's length to $T=100$ steps, which allows lead-time dynamics to repeat sufficiently while keeping training tractable.

\paragraph{\textbf{Demand.}} In the baseline, we set demand parameters such that the expected per-period demand for each \gls{acr:sku} in each region is $4 \cdot 0.667 \cdot 5 \approx 13$ units by \autoref{eq:expected-demand}. We chose this specific rate as it places demand in a regime that is neither near-deterministic nor near-Gaussian. Specifically, lead-time demand fluctuates enough from one replenishment cycle to the next so that the inventory decision is non-trivial, but stays discrete enough for individual integer orders to still matter.

\paragraph{\textbf{Costs.}} Because holding and variable shipment costs are both charged per unit weight, we uniformly fix $w_k=1$ for every \gls{acr:sku} in the symmetric baseline to avoid cost asymmetries. Moreover, we calibrate shipment costs from real freight data under a stylized home-versus-foreign topology, in which each warehouse sits $50$ km from its home region and $500$ km from each foreign region. In line with the distance-based cost regimes of the data, this yields $c^{\mathrm{in}}_{ik}=1.0$ per unit weight and an outbound variable rate $c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij}=0.05$ per unit weight for home shipments and $c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij}=0.5$ for foreign shipments. Additionally, we set both inbound and outbound fixed order costs to zero to avoid an order-batching incentive that would aggregate cost across \glspl{acr:sku} and confound the heterogeneity dimensions. For the full cost regimes and additional information on the real-world data, we refer to \autoref{ap:real-data}. Unlike the shipment costs, the holding rate $c^{\mathrm{hold}}$ and the penalty rate $c^{\mathrm{pen}}_k$ are not directly observable in our real-world freight data and must therefore be manually calibrated. Specifically, we set the penalty as five times the holding rate to obtain a newsvendor critical fraction of $c^{\mathrm{pen}}/(c^{\mathrm{pen}}+c^{\mathrm{hold}})\approx 0.83$ [\textbf{CITE}]. 
Note that we deliberately chose a lower level compared to the $0.90-0.95$ targets common in operational practice [\textbf{CITE}]. Because at higher service levels the penalty dominates and a simple high-stock heuristic is already near-optimal, calibrating $c^{\mathrm{hold}}$ and $c^{\mathrm{pen}}_k$ to achieve a lower fraction ensures that both over- and under-stocking remain costly so that the learned policy must genuinely trade off the two errors rather than collapse to a corner solution.

\paragraph{\textbf{Derived Quantities.}} The adjustment range $\Delta_k$ and the initial inventory $I_{ik,0}$ follow directly from the rules fixed in \autoref{chap: methodology}. Additionally, we report the expected fraction of demand $\kappa$ the BS-Optimized baseline fulfills cross-region, which quantifies how strongly the warehouses interact under the 10$\times$ cross-region cost premium.\\

\subsection{Heterogeneous Variants}
\label{subsec:heterogeneous-variants}
Before specifying each heterogeneous variant, we must ensure that any performance difference reflects the heterogeneity dimension alone rather than a change in raw problem size. For instance, a variant that simply moves more goods through the network would report different costs for unrelated reasons and thus confounds the comparison. Therefore, we hold the expected weighted network throughput per step constant across all five environments. Formally, this expected throughput is given by 
\autoref{eq:expected-demand}, this throughput is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:throughput-invariant}
  \bar{D} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} w_k\, \mu^D_{jk}
    = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} w_k\, \lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}_{j}\, p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}\, \lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}.
\end{equation}
Note that $\bar{D}$ fixes only the mean of demand and not its variance. However, because the order-level process is compound Poisson, the demand variance depends on the same rates $\lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}{j}, p^{\mathrm{sku}}{j}, \lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$ via \autoref{eq:demand-variance} and therefore shifts with the dimension that each variant modifies. Rather than adding a separate dispersion control, we retain this natural coupling for consistency with \autoref{sec:demand-model} and return to its consequences for interpretation in [\textbf{REF: limitations}]. Since in the symmetric environment $\bar{D}$ evaluates to $\approx200.1$ weight-units per step, each heterogeneous variant is constructed to maintain this value consistently.

\paragraph{\textbf{\gls{acr:sku} Heterogeneity} (SKUHetero).} This variant makes the \gls{acr:sku}
portfolio heterogeneous while keeping warehouses and geography symmetric. We assign log-spaced weights
$w=[0.3,1,3,10,30]$ over two orders of magnitude, which matches the practical span of a distribution catalogue
from small consumer goods to large appliances. To preserve the throughput invariant we set the per-line quantity
rates inversely proportional to weight, $\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_k=c/w_k$, so that heavier items move in smaller
per-order quantities as in real catalogues and the weighted demand of every \gls{acr:sku} is equal. Substituting
this rule into \autoref{eq:throughput-invariant} cancels the weights and fixes $c=5$ at the reference throughput.
The log-spacing is the deliberate stressor. No single global normalization can recover the correct magnitude for
both the lightest and the heaviest \gls{acr:sku} at once, and the thirtyfold weight span drives the per-\gls{acr:sku}
newsvendor service level from roughly $0.94$ for the lightest \gls{acr:sku} to roughly $0.14$ for the heaviest,
because holding cost scales with weight while the penalty does not. The portfolio therefore demands qualitatively
different behaviour per \gls{acr:sku}. Because the heaviest \glspl{acr:sku} draw fewer than one unit per line on
average, we keep the untruncated Poisson quantity of \autoref{sec:demand-model} so that the inverse-weight relation
and the throughput invariant hold exactly. The adjustment range and initial inventory follow \autoref{eq:delta-rule}
and \autoref{eq:initial-inventory} applied to the new demand and are reported in \autoref{tab:env-hetero}.

\paragraph{\textbf{Demand Heterogeneity} (DemandHetero).} This variant makes the per-warehouse demand scale
heterogeneous while keeping \glspl{acr:sku} and geography symmetric. We set the regional order rates to
$\lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}=[7,4,1]$, which sum to the same $12$ as the reference $[4,4,4]$ and therefore preserve the
throughput invariant because the remaining factors are unchanged. The triple represents a primary-versus-tail
pattern in which one region carries the majority of demand and the others handle progressively smaller shares,
typical of continental networks where demand concentrates in one mature market. The sevenfold span between the
busiest and the quietest region is large enough to expose a shared policy's inability to differentiate warehouses
by scale, and the quietest region at one order per step leaves its home warehouse facing frequent zero-demand
steps. The adjustment range, which takes the maximum expected demand over warehouses in \autoref{eq:delta-rule},
and the per-warehouse initial inventories follow from the new demand and are reported in \autoref{tab:env-hetero}.

\paragraph{\textbf{Cost Heterogeneity} (CostHetero).} This variant makes the shipment geography heterogeneous
while keeping demand and \glspl{acr:sku} symmetric. We replace the two-distance layout with a three-distance one of
$50$ km, $200$ km, and $5000$ km, with corresponding outbound rates $0.05$, $0.15$, and $1.5$ per unit weight under
the same freight calibration as the reference. This places two warehouse-region pairs on one continent about $200$
km apart and a third pair on a second continent about $5000$ km away. The threefold and thirtyfold rate ratios over
the home cost make cross-continent fulfillment economically prohibitive for the offshore warehouse while leaving
moderate cross-fulfillment available to the two same-continent warehouses. The three warehouses therefore occupy
distinct cost geographies that a shared policy must resolve from its observation. All demand-dependent quantities
are unchanged from the reference because no demand rate or weight is altered.

\paragraph{\textbf{Combined Heterogeneity} (CombinedHetero).} This variant activates all three axes at once.
It takes the log-spaced weights and inverse-weight quantity rates of the \gls{acr:sku} variant, the $[7,4,1]$ order
rates of the demand variant, and the three-distance cost geography of the cost variant. The throughput invariant
still holds because each axis preserves it independently and the construction in \autoref{eq:throughput-invariant}
composes. This is the highest-stress environment and the primary target for the combined intervention studied later.
Because both the \gls{acr:sku} weights and the regional order rates now vary, the adjustment ranges and the
per-warehouse, per-\gls{acr:sku} initial inventories take their widest spread of any variant, reported in full in
\autoref{tab:env-hetero}.
