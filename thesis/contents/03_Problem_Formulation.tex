\chapter{Problem Formulation}
\label{chap:problem-formulation}

Building on the limitations identified in the existing literature, we focus on a stylized yet realistic intra-company supply chain. The setting is inspired by a real-world case in which a manufacturing firm operates multiple geographically distributed warehouses that use independent \gls{acr:erp} systems and therefore make replenishment decisions without full visibility of each others' inventories or actions. In fact, this kind of decentralization is not a single exception but the operating reality of many large distribution networks, where heterogeneous information systems expose no shared real-time view of the network state and the integrated central planner assumed by classical multi-location inventory theory is therefore not readily available \citep{mousa2024analysis}.

The remainder of this chapter formalizes this setting as a discrete-time, multi-agent inventory control problem with stochastic demand, stochastic fulfillment, and lost sales. \autoref{sec:network-structure} fixes the static structure of the network and the sections that follow then trace the flow of a single period. \autoref{sec:demand-model} specifies how customer demand is generated at the level of individual orders. \autoref{sec:fulfillment} describes how that demand is served from inventory, how warehouses help each other through transshipment, and how unmet demand becomes lost sales. \autoref{sec:replenishment} explains how warehouses replenish their stock from external suppliers under a fixed lead time. \autoref{sec:replenishment} collects these elements into the replenishment and inventory dynamics, and \autoref{sec:costs} defines the cost structure and the system objective. \\

\section{Network Structure}
\label{sec:network-structure}
We model the problem as an intra-organizational, single-echelon inventory control setting that consists of set of geographically distributed warehouses $\mathcal{W} = \{1, \dots, W\}$ and a set of distinct demand regions $\mathcal{R} = \{1, \dots, R\}$. We index warehouses by $i$ and regions by $j$ and assume throughout that $W=R$ so that each warehouse can be assigned a distinct home demand region. Warehouse $i$ and region $j=i$ are thus co-located and no other warehouse $i'\ne i$ has the same home demand region $i$. Nevertheless, we keep the warehouse index $i$ and the region index $j$ separate in the notation to emphasize that warehouses and regions are conceptually different objects. Although each warehouse is assigned a primary demand region, the network is coupled through a fully connected transshipment network that permits cross-regional demand fulfillment. All warehouses in the network stock the same set of \glspl{acr:sku} $\mathcal{K} = \{1, \dots, K\}$, where each \gls{acr:sku} $k$ has unit weight $w_k$ and can be replenished from a separate uncontrolled upstream supply. Therefore, our setting treats both downstream distribution and upstream supply as exogenous and focuses solely on intra-firm replenishment dynamics. A visual representation of the network structure is provided in \autoref{fig:sc-network}. The system evolves according to a fixed decision sequence. Within each  discrete time step $t$, the following chain of events is repeated for all warehouses simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each warehouse $i$ places replenishment orders for all \glspl{acr:sku} $k$ at
  \gls{acr:sku}-specific external suppliers. Suppliers are assumed to be uncapacitated and each replenishment order arrives after a fixed delivery lead time.
  \item Warehouse $i$ receives previously placed replenishment orders according to the fixed delivery lead time and add them to its on-hand inventory
  \item Warehouse $i$ receives new orders. Each order contains demand for one or multiple \glspl{acr:sku} and is drawn from a stochastic demand process.
  \item arehouse $i$ fulfills incoming orders according to its on-hand inventory. If inventory is not sufficient for a given order, residual demand is fulfilled by other warehouses, when possible. Any unfulfilled demand results in lost sales. 
\end{enumerate}

\section{Demand Process}
\label{sec:demand-model}
The network faces stochastic demand for each of the $K$ \glspl{acr:sku} that is realized across the $R$ demand regions. Most prior multi-agent inventory studies represent this demand as a single aggregate rate per \gls{acr:sku} \citep{mousa2024analysis}. In contrast, we model demand at the level of individual customer orders, where a single order may request several \glspl{acr:sku} at once. We adopt this order-level representation for two reasons. First, it brings the environment closer to operational practice since real customers place multi-line orders rather than emitting a smooth per-\gls{acr:sku} stream. Second, it decomposes demand into separate components that can be controlled independently, which we exploit later to design environment variants.

To be able to simulate order-level demand, we model the generation process as a multistage procedure in which order arrivals, basket composition, and quantities are drawn sequentially.  Specifically, we compute the demand for region $j$ in period $t$ as follows. First, we sample the number of customer orders $N^{\mathrm{ord}}_{jt} = |\mathcal{C}_{jt}|$ from a Poisson distribution with region-specific arrival rate $\lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}_{j}$, where $\mathcal{C}_{jt}$ denotes the set of arriving customer orders. For each order $o\in\mathcal{C}_{jt}$ and every \gls{acr:sku} $k$, we then independently draw an inclusion indicator $Z^{o}_{jkt}$ from a Bernoulli distribution with region-specific probability $p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}$ to determine whether order $o$ contains \gls{acr:sku} $k$. Finally, we sample the requested quantity $Q^{o}_{jkt}$ for each included \gls{acr:sku} $k$ independently from a Poisson distribution with region and \gls{acr:sku} specific rate $\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$. In summary, the demand generation process is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:demand-process}
\begin{aligned}
  N^{\mathrm{ord}}_{jt} &\sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}_{j}), \\
  Z^{o}_{jkt}           &\sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}), \\
  Q^{o}_{jkt}           &\sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
independently across orders $o \in \mathcal{C}_{jt}$ and \glspl{acr:sku} $k$. Note that we index $p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}$ by region alone since $\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$ already carries \gls{acr:sku}-level demand variation and a per-\gls{acr:sku} inclusion probability would thus be redundant for the mean. Given the process in \autoref{eq:demand-process}, it follows that the demand for \gls{acr:sku} $k$ in order $o$ is $D^{o}_{jkt} = Z^{o}_{jkt}\,Q^{o}_{jkt}$ and summing over all orders $o \in \mathcal{C}_{jt}$ yields the total period demand for \gls{acr:sku} $k$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total-demand}
  D_{jkt} = \sum_{o \in \mathcal{C}_{jt}} D^{o}_{jkt}.
\end{equation}

In formulating the demand model above, we rely on three modelling assumptions. First, we consider the stationary case where the demand rates $\lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}_{j}$, $p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}$, and $\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$ are constant across periods such that there are no trends within an episode. Second, we model all random variables in \autoref{eq:demand-process} as mutually independent across orders and across \glspl{acr:sku} within an order, which directly implies the absence of cross-\gls{acr:sku} correlation. Finally, we model both the order count and the per-line quantity as Poisson such that each has variance equal to its mean.
As a result, $D_{jkt}$ follows a compound Poisson law and, by the law of total expectation, its mean factorizes cleanly into the three rate parameters, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expected-demand}
   \mu^D_{jk} := \mathbb{E}[D_{jkt}] = \lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}_{j}\,p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}\,
  \lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{jk}$ is independent of the period $t$ because the three demand rates are constant across periods. Each rate thus acts as one multiplicative factor such that changing one of them scales expected demand proportionally while leaving the other two untouched. Therefore, the three rate variables are the only free parameters of the demand process, which is exactly the property we use to reliably construct heterogeneous variants of the environment later. As with the mean, the same compound Poisson structure combined with the law of total variance give the variance of $D_{jkt}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:demand-variance}
  \mathrm{Var}[D_{jkt}] = \lambda^{\mathrm{ord}}_{j}\,p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}\,\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}\,\bigl(1+\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus, it can be directly seen that in the compound model the variance exceeds the mean by a factor $1+\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$ such that the order-level demand is over-dispersed relative to a simple per-\gls{acr:sku} Poisson stream. This over-dispersion arises directly from the compound structure of the model, in which random per-line quantities are bundled into a random number of orders. We retain this property since we do not regard it as a shortcoming but rather as a more faithful reflection of real demand, which is commonly over-dispersed rather than clean Poisson \citep{kourentzes2013intermittent}. 

Note that in the demand-generation process as defined above an included \gls{acr:sku} with $Z^{o}_{jkt} = 1$ may still draw a zero quantity such that $D^{o}_{jkt} = 0$. To address this, one could either sample $Q^{o}_{jkt}$ from a zero-truncated Poisson or impose a lower bound of one unit on all included quantities. However, we deliberately keep the untruncated Poisson. Forcing positivity would inflate the expected quantity per included \gls{acr:sku} above $\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$ and make $\lambda^{\mathrm{qty}}_{jk}$ enter \autoref{eq:expected-demand} as a nonlinear function of itself rather than as a clean multiplicative factor. In our model, the realized assortment of an order is instead the set of \glspl{acr:sku} with a strictly positive quantity rather than the full set of included \glspl{acr:sku}.

The price of this choice is purely interpretational. Because inclusion no longer coincides with appearance, $p^{\mathrm{sku}}_{j}$ is only a nominal inclusion probability rather than the fraction of orders in which a \gls{acr:sku} appears with positive quantity. We accept this deviation because it leaves the factorization in \autoref{eq:expected-demand} unaffected such that the three rate parameters retain their multiplicative roles in expected demand.\\

\section{Demand Fulfillment and Lost Sales}
\label{sec:fulfillment}
Once region demand has been generated, warehouses must fulfill it from their inventories. Since the network is fully connected and the agents control only replenishment rather than routing, the decision of which warehouse supplies each order is made by the system rather than learned. We model this assignment with a greedy rule, which we adopt for its simplicity and because a cheapest-first dispatch is a reasonable myopic stand-in for how a firm routes orders without central fulfillment planning.

The greedy allocator processes orders one at a time. Let $I_{ikt}$ denote the on-hand inventory of \gls{acr:sku} $k$ at warehouse $i$ available for fulfillment in period $t$ and let $c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij}$ denote the outbound shipment cost per unit of weight from warehouse $i$ to region~$j$. We assume no per-order fixed costs such that an order's shipment cost scales purely with the weight shipped. For each order $o \in \mathcal{C}_{jt}$, the allocator ranks the warehouses by their outbound shipment cost to region $j$ and tries them cheapest first. Each warehouse in turn fulfills the requested \gls{acr:sku} up to its on-hand inventory $I_{ikt}$. and any demand that it cannot cover passes to the next-cheapest warehouse in the ranking. This continues until the order is fully served or all warehouses have been tried. Note that the cost ranking turns the home-region structure introduced in \autoref{sec:network-structure} into a fulfillment priority. Because $c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij}$ scales with distance, every warehouse ships to its home region more cheaply than any other warehouse ships to a foreign region. The home warehouse is therefore tried first for each order from its own region and a foreign warehouse is reached only when the home warehouse cannot supply the requested \gls{acr:sku}. The greedy allocator produces the shipment quantities $S_{ijkt}$ that warehouse $i$ ships to region $j$ in period $t$. Since a warehouse can never ship more of a \gls{acr:sku} than it holds, $S_{ijkt}$ must satisfy,
  \begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shipment-feasibility}
  \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} S_{ijkt} \le I_{ikt}, 
\end{equation}
for all warehouses $i$, \glspl{acr:sku} $k$, and periods $t$. Any shipment $S_{ijkt}$ with $j \ne i$ serves a foreign region and is called a lateral transshipment. We model such a transshipment as a direct shipment from warehouse $i$ to region $j$ rather than a transfer through region $j$'s home warehouse such that the unit travels the full distance and is charged the higher foreign-region rate $c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij}$ with no separate transfer cost. Moreover, transshipment is entirely reactive rather than predictive [\textbf{CITE: reactive vs.\ proactive lateral transshipment taxonomy}] and is not an active decision by the warehouses. More generally, because the allocator clears each order against the inventories of all warehouses at once, the entire shipment array is a function of their joint on-hand inventory rather than of any single warehouse's  stock,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:allocation-map}
  \{S_{ijkt}\}_{i,j,k} = \mathcal{F}\!\left(\{ \mathcal{C}_{jt}\}_{j},\, \{I_{ikt}\}_{i,k}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the greedy rule described above. This joint dependence is the channel through which the warehouses are coupled since a stockout at one warehouse raises the transshipments that its neighbors must make, even though each warehouse controls only its own replenishment.

Any demand that the allocator cannot place is lost such that there are no backorders. The lost demand of \gls{acr:sku} $k$ in region $j$ is therefore the portion of its total demand $D_{jkt}$ that no warehouse fills,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lost-sales}
  U_{jkt} = D_{jkt} - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{W}} S_{ijkt} \ \ge\ 0,
\end{equation}
where the inequality holds because the allocator never ships more than was demands. Each warehouse then incurs a per\gls{acr:sku} penalty rate $c^{\mathrm{pen}}_k$ for each lost unit that it is responsible for. However, while responsibility is clear when a single warehouse serves a region, it becomes equivocal whenever a region is served by multiple warehouses. We resolve this ambiguity with a shipment-proportional rule that assigns the lost demand of a region in proportion to the volume that each warehouse shipped to that region. 
Specifically, the share of region $j$'s lost demand assigned to warehouse $i$  is given by 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attribution-weight}
  \beta_{ijt} =
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{S_{ijt}}{\sum_{i' \in \mathcal{W}} S_{i'jt}} & \text{if } \sum_{i' \in \mathcal{W}} S_{i'jt} > 0, \\[2.2ex]
    \mathbf{1}\{\,i = j\,\} & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $S_{ijt} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} S_{ijkt}$ is the total volume that warehouse $i$ shipped to region $j$ across all \glspl{acr:sku}. The first case splits lost sales in proportion to participation such that the warehouse that supplied most of a region's volume also absorbs most of its loss. The second case covers a region that received no shipments in which case the whole loss falls to its home warehouse $i=j$. 
Aggregating shares across regions then yields warehouse $i$'s total attributed lost sales, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:attributed-lost-sales}
  U^{\mathrm{attr}}_{ikt} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} \beta_{ijt}\, U_{jkt}.
\end{equation}
Note that the weight $\beta_{ijt}$ is computed from shipped volume aggregated over \glspl{acr:sku} and is then applied to the loss of every \gls{acr:sku} such that the attribution is decided per warehouse-region pair rather than per \gls{acr:sku}.
Since $\beta_{ijt}$ sums to one across warehouses, the attribution preserves the total lost demand of each \gls{acr:sku} and yields $\sum_{i \in \mathcal{W}} U^{\mathrm{attr}}_{ikt} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} U_{jkt}$.\\

\section{Replenishment and Inventory Dynamics}
\label{sec:replenishment}
Since fulfilling demand depletes inventory, warehouses need to continually replenish their stock. To do so, in every period $t$ each warehouse $i$ chooses order quantities $q_{ikt} \ge 0$ and places them with an external suppliers. We assume that each \gls{acr:sku} $k$ has its own dedicated supplier such that the warehouses face no sourcing decision. Moreover, each supplier is uncapacitated and accepts each order in full. Hence, no two warehouses compete with another for supply so that every order is accepted in full and no two warehouses compete for supply and the replenishment of one warehouse never constrains another. As with the outbound case, each \gls{acr:sku} replenishment order that warehouse $i$ places incurs an inbound cost $c^{\mathrm{in}}_{ik}$ per unit of weight ordered. We again assume no fixed costs such that the replenishment cost of an order scales purely with the ordered weight.

Replenishment orders do not arrive in the period in which they are placed but are instead subject to a fixed lead time $L_{ik}$ such that an order placed in period $t$ arrives at the start of period $t + L_{ik}$. The quantity $A_{ikt}$ of \gls{acr:sku} $k$ that arrives at warehouse $i$ in period $t$ is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:arrivals}
  A_{ikt} = q_{ik,\,t-L_{ik}},
\end{equation}
where any order with a non-positive time index is zero. Between placement and arrival the ordered units are in transit and are thus not yet available to the warehouse. We refer to the total quantity that warehouse $i$ has ordered for \gls{acr:sku} $k$ but not yet received as the inventory in transit and define it as,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pipeline}
  I^{tr}_{ikt} = \sum_{s = t - L_{ik} + 1}^{t} q_{iks},
\end{equation}

Since arrivals add stock and fulfillment removes it, on-hand inventory naturally evolves over time. Specifically, the inventory available to warehouse $i$ for \gls{acr:sku} $k$ evolves as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inventory-dynamics}
  I_{ik,t+1} = I_{ikt} - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} S_{ijkt} + A_{ik,t+1},
\end{equation}
which remains non-negative due to the feasibility constraint in \autoref{eq:shipment-feasibility}. In the special case of $t=0$, each warehouse starts with a configured initial inventory $I_{ik,0}$ and empty pipeline (see \autoref{subsec:initial-settings}). Likewise, in the case of $t=T$, any order with $t + L_{ik} > T$ will never arrive and is simply discarded. The complete physical state of the network at any period is therefore the on-hand inventory together with the pipeline of every warehouse and \gls{acr:sku}.\\


\section{Cost Structure and Objective}
\label{sec:costs}
At the end of each period $t$, every warehouse $i$ is subject to a range of costs. First, holding inventory is typically associated with extra fees such as storage, cleaning, electricity, etc., so that each warehouse pays a holding cost on excess inventory, which is defined as,  
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:holding-cost}
  C^{\mathrm{hold}}_{it} = c^{\mathrm{hold}} \sum_k w_k\, I_{ik,t+1},
\end{equation}
where $c^{\mathrm{hold}}$ is the holding rate per unit weight per period. Since holding cost scales with weight, stocking one unit of a heavier \glspl{acr:sku} is proportionally more expensive than one unit of a lighter item.

Second, to avoid lost revenue due to unmet demand, each warehouse is penalized for each lost unit of demand attributed to it and thus pay a penalty charge as follows,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:penalty-cost}
  C^{\mathrm{pen}}_{it} = \sum_k c^{\mathrm{pen}}_k\, U^{\mathrm{attr}}_{ikt},
\end{equation}
where $c^{\mathrm{pen}}_k$ is the per-\gls{acr:sku} penalty rate. Unlike the holding cost, the penalty does not scale with weight since it reflects the lost value of an unserved unit of demand rather than a physical handling cost.

Third, for every unit shipped during fulfillment, a warehouse has to pay outbound shipment costs, defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:outbound-cost}
  C^{\mathrm{out}}_{it} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}}
  c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij} \, w_k \, S_{ijkt},
\end{equation}
where $c^{\mathrm{out}}_{ij}$ is the outbound rate per unit of weight. Since the sum runs over all regions, the outbound cost includes both the shipments a warehouse makes to its own home region and the transshipments it makes to foreign regions.

Finally, when placing replenishment orders, each warehouse has to pay procurement costs, given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inbound-cost}
  C^{\mathrm{in}}_{it} = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} c^{\mathrm{in}}_{ik} \, w_k \,
  q_{ikt},
\end{equation}
where $c^{\mathrm{in}}_{ik}$ is the inbound rate per unit of weight. Note that inbound costs are incured in the period the order is placed rather than when the order arrives. Like the holding and outbound costs, it scales with weight such that a heavier \gls{acr:sku} is more expensive to replenish.

Summing the four components gives the total cost of warehouse $i$ in period $t$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:warehouse-cost}
  C_{it} = C^{\mathrm{hold}}_{it} + C^{\mathrm{pen}}_{it} + C^{\mathrm{out}}_{it}
  + C^{\mathrm{in}}_{it},
\end{equation}

Aggregating $C_{it}$ across warehouses then yields the the total system cost in period $t$, determined by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:system-cost}
  C_{t} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{W}} C_{it}.
\end{equation}

This system cost can be accumulated over the planning horizon $T$ to obtain the total supply chain costs, given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tscc}
  C = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} C_t,
\end{equation}
which we use as the headline performance measure of a policy throughout the thesis. Since
demand is stochastic, $C$ is a random variable and the control objective is to minimize
its expectation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
  \min\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[C\right].
\end{equation}

Finding a policy that minimizes this objective is highly non-trivial due to the decentralized information structure and the compound stochasticity of the demand, which makes it necessary to define an algorithmic framework that is capable of efficiently solving this setting.
